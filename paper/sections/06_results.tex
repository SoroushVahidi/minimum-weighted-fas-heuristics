\section{Computational results}
\label{sec:results}

The computational evidence should be read in the same order as the experimental design. The paper first establishes the main sparse-benchmark result, then checks exact small-instance quality, then uses ablations to interpret the engineering choices, and finally tests transfer to dense LOLIB instances. This ordering matters because the paper's positive claim is intentionally narrow: IPSNS is presented as a strong, reproducible heuristic framework for nonnegative sparse weighted directed graphs, not as a universal best method for every ordering benchmark.

\subsection{Sparse weighted graph benchmarks}

The primary result comes from the standard nonnegative sparse benchmark in EXP4, with EXP1b supplying the internal safety check. The main outcome is unambiguous on this benchmark. IPSNS achieves the lowest observed backward weight on 96 of the 97 standard instances. Its mean BW is 37,698, compared with 38,327 for LR-TA and 40,005 for WMSF. Among the external methods, DRMaciver is clearly the strongest comparator, but its mean BW is still 53,173 on the completed standard instances, or about 21.61\% above IPSNS. The remaining baselines are substantially weaker on average, with the Eades variants about 30\% higher than IPSNS and the simpler Borda and random baselines far behind.

This result addresses the baseline-strength concern directly. The sparse-benchmark claim is not based only on a comparison among internal variants. It survives contact with a strong external comparator, a widely available graph-library heuristic, and several simple but informative ranking baselines. The most important external comparison is DRMaciver, not because it wins the benchmark, but because it is the only external method that remains genuinely competitive on a large portion of the sparse set. Even there, the gap is small only on one instance: \texttt{r20\_60}, where DRMaciver beats IPSNS by 3 BW units. That same instance is the only IPSNS near-miss in the exact-validation experiment below, which makes the sparse story internally consistent rather than contradictory.

EXP1b strengthens this interpretation by showing that the refinement stage does not obtain these gains by occasionally damaging its seeds. On the full 105-instance internal sparse benchmark, IPSNS is never worse than LR-TA and never worse than WMSF, with zero incumbent-protection violations and zero nonempty errors in the committed summary. It strictly improves over LR-TA on 16 instances and over WMSF on 36 instances. The mean runtime per instance rises from 0.074 seconds for LR-TA and 1.24 seconds for WMSF to about 20.2 seconds for IPSNS, so the gain is not free; however, it is gained without giving up the best internal seed. That property is more informative than a generic "hybrid beats components" statement because it connects the empirical behavior back to the monotone non-degradation invariant in Section~\ref{sec:algorithmic-framework}.

The runtime pattern also clarifies the engineering tradeoff. LR-TA is the fastest strong method in the study: its mean sparse-benchmark runtime is only 0.08 seconds per instance, yet its mean BW is within about 1.7\% of IPSNS. That makes LR-TA a credible low-latency choice when refinement time is limited. IPSNS then occupies the higher-quality point on the tradeoff curve, paying more runtime to secure the best observed solutions on almost every instance. For a CAIE framing, that is the right practical interpretation: the paper provides a fast seed heuristic, a stronger refinement framework, and a clear statement of what the extra computation buys.

The sparse results also answer the concern that the paper should say more about dataset breadth without pretending to have solved every graph family. The benchmark is not tiny, synthetic-only, or internal-only. It contains 97 standard nonnegative sparse instances after exclusions, and the external comparison includes eight algorithms in total. At the same time, the claim remains intentionally scoped to this sparse nonnegative setting. The paper does not generalize from this table to dense complete ordering benchmarks or to negative-weight cases.

\subsection{Exact validation on small instances}

Table~\ref{tab:exact-validation} reports the small-instance exact validation from EXP3.

\input{tables/table_exact_validation}

The main point of this experiment is quality validation, not theoretical elevation. On the 57 standard nonnegative instances with \(n \le 20\), IPSNS is exactly optimal on 56 and has a mean gap of 0.0006\% from the exact optimum. LR-TA is optimal on 55 instances with mean gap 0.0590\%, and WMSF is optimal on 51 with mean gap 0.0961\%. Those results show that the heuristic framework is not merely competitive in relative benchmark terms; on small instances where exact optimization is available, it is essentially exact in practice.

The wording matters here. This table does not provide an approximation guarantee, a worst-case bound, or a proof of global behavior beyond the validated subset. The rejection audit identified that distinction as a necessary claim boundary, and the experiment supports it naturally. The correct statement is that IPSNS is empirically near-optimal on the exact-validation subset. That statement is already strong enough for the role this experiment is meant to play.

The exact-validation result also helps interpret the single sparse-benchmark loss to DRMaciver. The only IPSNS near-miss is again \texttt{r20\_60}, which is also the only standard small instance where IPSNS fails to match exact optimum. That consistency reduces the chance that the sparse EXP4 story is driven by unstable reporting or artifact mismatch. Instead, it suggests a small, structurally difficult case where the framework is extremely close to optimum but not uniquely dominant.

\subsection{Ablation study}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the ablation results from EXP2.

\input{tables/table_ablation}

The add-back phase is the dominant contributor among the tested design choices. Removing it raises mean BW from 4,271.5 for LR-TA to 4,525.1 for the no-add-back variant, a degradation of about 5.94\%. That is the clearest experimental support for treating topological add-back as a substantive engineering contribution rather than a decorative post-processing step.

The refinement stage then provides a smaller but still measurable gain. Moving from LR-TA to IPSNS with 50 iterations reduces mean BW from 4,271.5 to 4,239.2, about 0.76\%. In absolute terms that gain is much smaller than the add-back gain, but it is still meaningful because it is obtained after starting from a strong seed and while preserving the non-degradation invariant. That is exactly the kind of marginal improvement one expects from a bounded refinement layer: it does not re-invent the seed; it improves the hard residue that remains after seeding.

The ablation also provides the manuscript's main evidence for parameter justification, and here the interpretation must stay conservative. On this 10-instance subset, the 50-iteration and 200-iteration IPSNS variants have the same mean BW, which suggests that the default budget is already past the point of visible average improvement on these instances. Likewise, removing SCC priority changes the mean only from 4,239.2 to 4,239.1, effectively negligible at the reported precision. These observations support the default settings as reasonable committed choices, but they do not prove that those settings are universally optimal on other graph families or scales. The paper should therefore use EXP2 to justify the defaults operationally, not to claim an exhaustive tuning study.

Finally, the ablation table addresses one subtle rejection concern about topological and local-ratio design choices. The results show that the manuscript's main engineering claims are not all bundled into one opaque black box. The strongest effect comes from add-back, the refinement effect is positive but smaller, and the SCC-priority heuristic appears locally minor on this subset. That is the kind of decomposition a computational journal expects from a heuristic paper that wants to argue for design, not just for outcomes.

\subsection{Dense LOLIB transfer test}

Table~\ref{tab:lolib-scope} and Figure~\ref{fig:exp5-lolib-scope} summarize the dense LOLIB transfer test from EXP5.

    \caption{Global-best counts by LOLIB family for IPSNS and DRMaciver. The family split makes the dense-scope boundary explicit: DRMaciver dominates overall, while IPSNS remains competitive on the IO family.}
    \label{fig:exp5-lolib-scope}
\end{figure}

This is the experiment that prevents overclaiming. On the 50 dense complete ordering instances, DRMaciver is the best overall method in the study: it wins 45 instances and achieves mean BW 571,687, compared with 582,354 for IPSNS. The mean gap is 3.88\% in DRMaciver's favor. IPSNS remains second overall and retains zero incumbent violations against LR-TA and WMSF, but the dense benchmark does not support any claim of overall dominance by the proposed framework.

The family breakdown explains why. DRMaciver wins 24 of the 25 SGB instances and all 15 RandA1 instances. IPSNS is more competitive on IO, where it wins 4 of 10 against DRMaciver's 6 of 10, but that does not overturn the overall dense-ordering result. The cleanest interpretation is structural: DRMaciver is tournament-native, whereas IPSNS is designed around sparse weighted-digraph seeding and SCC-local refinement. When every ordered pair carries weight and the instance is fully dense, the advantage shifts toward methods specialized for that structure.

This finding is important precisely because it narrows the manuscript's claim in a credible way. A paper that only reported the sparse benchmark could be challenged for hiding unfavorable regimes. By retaining EXP5 and writing it honestly, the manuscript does the opposite. It shows that the framework transfers nontrivially to a dense benchmark, remains competitive on a structured IO subset, and still preserves the internal no-worsening invariant, but it also shows that dense complete ordering is not the framework's strongest regime.

That conclusion should not be framed defensively. It improves the paper's scientific significance because it turns a potential objection into a useful empirical boundary. The result is not "our method fails on LOLIB"; it is "our method is strong on sparse weighted digraphs, bounded on dense complete ordering problems, and transparently so." For CAIE, that is a better contribution than a broader but less defensible universal claim.

\subsection{Overall interpretation}

Taken together, the experiments support three manuscript-level conclusions.

First, the strongest claim belongs to the sparse nonnegative benchmark. There, IPSNS is the best observed method on 96 of 97 standard instances, improves materially over external baselines, and never returns a result worse than the better internal seed.

Second, the quality story is supported independently of the external baseline story. EXP3 shows near-optimality on the exact small-instance subset, and EXP2 shows that the largest design contribution comes from topological add-back, with refinement providing an additional but smaller gain.

Third, the manuscript's limitations are empirically visible rather than merely admitted in prose. EXP5 shows the dense complete-ordering boundary directly, and the sparse benchmark exclusions keep the claim surface aligned with the nonnegative setting actually studied. This combination is what allows the paper to be assertive without overclaiming: the framework is a strong and reproducible method for the sparse weighted-digraph regime, and the experiments show exactly how far that statement can be pushed.
